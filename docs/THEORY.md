# Theory and Concepts

*A review of the mathematical background, the structures carried by our data
primals, and the interactions between those structures.*

---

## 0. Scope, method, and how to read this

**This document proposes nothing.** It reviews established theory, identifies
which structures our primitive data actually carry, and studies how those
structures interact. No design recommendation appears anywhere. Work built on
this review belongs elsewhere.

**Meaning is bounded by detectability.** Every semantic notion used here is
grounded in the detectable content space enumerated in
`CONTENT_EXTRACTION_RULES.md` — five categories, eighteen subcategories, and the
attribute and tag vocabularies listed there. Nothing outside that space has
meaning to any stage of the pipeline, so nothing outside it is modelled here. In
particular, this document never appeals to "the semantics of the page"; it
appeals only to what §2 of the rules document says can be observed.

**Structure of the review.**

- **Part I (§1–§9)** reviews the relevant fields. It is prerequisite and contains
  nothing specific to our data. Results marked **(standard)** are classical and
  cited; results marked **(elementary)** are proved, because the argument recurs.
- **Part II (§10–§17)** takes each *data primal* — the smallest datum the
  architecture manipulates — and identifies the minimal mathematical structure it
  carries, using only Part I.
- **Part III (§18–§23)** is abstractive: it studies the interactions *between*
  those structures — the morphisms, quotients, projections and derivatives that
  relate one carrier to another.
- **Part IV (§24)** reads the abstract results back down to the primal level,
  stating what each says about a single address, a single count, a single field.

Parts II and III are meant to be read in both directions. The upward direction
says what a datum forces; the downward direction says what an abstract fact costs
in concrete terms.

# Part I — Background

Prerequisite theory. Nothing here concerns our data.

## 1. Order and lattice theory

### 1.1 Sets

$[n] = \{1,\dots,n\}$; $\mathcal{P}(X)$ is the power set;
$X \triangle Y = (X\setminus Y)\cup(Y\setminus X)$. All sets here are finite,
which several results below require.

### 1.2 Relations, preorders, quotients

A relation $R \subseteq X\times X$ is **reflexive**, **symmetric**,
**antisymmetric**, **transitive** in the usual senses. An **equivalence** is
reflexive, symmetric, transitive; its classes $[x]$ partition $X$, giving the
**quotient** $X/{\equiv}$.

A **preorder** $\lesssim$ is reflexive and transitive. A **partial order** adds
antisymmetry. Every preorder induces an equivalence $x\equiv y \iff x\lesssim y
\wedge y\lesssim x$, and descends to a partial order on $X/{\equiv}$.

**Remark 1.2.1.** A symmetric relation records only *sameness*. An order records
*direction*. No equivalence relation, and hence no construction whose output is
an equivalence, can express a preference between two related elements. This is a
property of symmetry, not of any particular construction, and it recurs in §7.4
and §19.

### 1.3 Chains and antichains

A **chain** is pairwise comparable; an **antichain** pairwise incomparable.
$\max(S)$ is the set of elements of $S$ with no strict upper bound in $S$; for
finite nonempty $S$ it is nonempty and is an antichain.

**Theorem 1.3.1 (Dilworth) (standard).** Maximum antichain size equals minimum
chain-cover size. [Dilworth 1950.] **Theorem 1.3.2 (Mirsky) (standard).** Dually
for chains and antichain covers. [Mirsky 1971.]

### 1.4 Down-sets

$S$ is a **down-set** if $y\leq x\in S \Rightarrow y\in S$. The down-set
generated by $S$ is $\downarrow\! S=\{y:\exists x\in S,\ y\leq x\}$; $\downarrow$
is a closure operator.

**Proposition 1.4.1 (elementary).** For finite $P$, $A\mapsto\downarrow\! A$ and
$I\mapsto\max(I)$ are mutually inverse bijections between antichains and
down-sets.

*Proof.* Each $a\in A$ is maximal in $\downarrow\! A$ and each maximal element
lies below some $a$, hence equals it; so $\max(\downarrow\! A)=A$. Conversely
$\downarrow\!\max(I)\subseteq I$ by down-closure, and every $x\in I$ lies below a
maximal element by finiteness. $\square$

This bijection converts questions about incomparable families into questions
about ordinary sets, where §8 applies.

### 1.5 Lattices

A **lattice** has binary joins and meets; it is **distributive** if
$x\wedge(y\vee z)=(x\wedge y)\vee(x\wedge z)$. $\mathcal{O}(P)$, the down-sets of
$P$ under inclusion, is a distributive lattice with $\cup,\cap$.

**Theorem 1.5.1 (Birkhoff) (standard).** Every finite distributive lattice is
$\mathcal{O}(P)$ for $P$ its poset of join-irreducibles; finite posets and finite
distributive lattices are dual. [Birkhoff 1937.]

### 1.6 Forest orders

$(P,\leq)$ is a **forest order** if $\downarrow\!\{x\}$ is a chain for every $x$.
Equivalently every element has at most one immediate predecessor. In a forest
order comparability is decided by a single root-path test.

## 2. Semigroups, monoids, groups

### 2.1–2.2 Basic notions

A **semigroup** is an associative magma; a **monoid** $(M,\cdot,e)$ adds an
identity. Associativity is what permits evaluation in any bracketing, hence in
any traversal order. **Commutative** means order-free; **idempotent** means
$x\cdot x = x$, i.e. repetition is absorbed.

### 2.3 Homomorphisms and products

$f$ is a **homomorphism** if $f(e)=e$ and $f(xy)=f(x)f(y)$.

**Proposition 2.3.1 (elementary).** A componentwise product of monoids is a
monoid; a map into a product is a homomorphism iff each component is. $\square$

### 2.4 Free monoids

**Theorem 2.4.1 (universal property) (standard).** For any monoid $M$ and any
$f:\Sigma\to M$ there is a unique homomorphism $f^{*}:\Sigma^{*}\to M$ extending
$f$.

To define a homomorphism on sequences it suffices to define it on letters.

### 2.5 Groups

A **group** is a monoid with inverses. The computational content: any applied
operation is undoable in one step without consulting history.

### 2.6 Free commutative monoids

$\mathbb{N}^{(\Sigma)}$ is the set of finite-support $m:\Sigma\to\mathbb{N}$
under pointwise addition $\uplus$; elements are **finite multisets**;
$\mathrm{supp}(m)=\{a:m(a)>0\}$. **Multiset inclusion** is
$m\subseteq_m m'\iff\forall a.\,m(a)\leq m'(a)$.

**Proposition 2.6.1 (elementary).** $(\mathbb{N}^{(\Sigma)},\subseteq_m)$ is a
distributive lattice under pointwise $\min$ and $\max$. $\square$

**Remark 2.6.2.** A multiset carries *two* distinct structures — the monoid
$\uplus$ and the lattice order $\subseteq_m$ — and $\uplus\neq\vee$. Conflating
them is a standard error and is relevant at §14 and §19.

### 2.7 Cancellativity

$M$ is **cancellative** if $xz=yz\Rightarrow x=y$. $(\mathbb{N},+)$ is;
$(\mathbb{N},\max)$ is not ($3\max 3=1\max 3$); $(\mathcal{P}(X),\cup)$ is not.
Failure of cancellativity means the accumulated value does not determine enough
about what was accumulated to remove one contribution.

### 2.8 Group completion

**Theorem 2.8.1 (Grothendieck) (standard).** Every commutative monoid $M$ admits
a universal homomorphism $\iota:M\to\mathrm{Gr}(M)$ into an abelian group;
$\iota$ is injective **iff** $M$ is cancellative.

A non-cancellative aggregate embeds in no group, so no reformulation supplies
inverses.

### 2.9 Semirings

$(S,+,\cdot,0,1)$ with $+$ commutative, $\cdot$ distributing, $0$ annihilating.
The **tropical** semiring $(\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\},\min,+,\infty,0)$ is the
setting for minimum-cost recurrences.

## 3. Words, languages, machines

### 3.1–3.2 Words and regularity

$\Sigma$ finite; $\Sigma^{*}$ the free monoid; $|w|$ the length. A language is
regular iff recognised by a DFA; regular languages are closed under Boolean
operations, concatenation, star, reversal, and morphic image and preimage.

**Theorem 3.2.1 (Myhill–Nerode) (standard).** $L$ is regular iff its right
congruence has finite index; the minimal DFA has one state per class.

### 3.3 The prefix order

$u\preceq w \iff \exists v.\ w=uv$; then $v=u^{-1}w$ is unique.

**Proposition 3.3.1 (elementary).** $(\Sigma^{*},\preceq)$ is a forest order: the
prefixes of a word form a chain ordered by length. $\square$

### 3.4 Prefix codes, tries, Kraft

$C$ is a **prefix code** iff it is an antichain in $(\Sigma^{*},\preceq)$;
**maximal** if nothing can be added.

**Theorem 3.4.1 (Kraft; McMillan) (standard).** For a prefix code over $r$
letters with lengths $\ell_i$, $\sum_i r^{-\ell_i}\leq 1$, with equality iff
maximal; conversely any such lengths are realisable. [Kraft 1949; McMillan 1956.]

A **trie** over $S$ has the prefixes of $S$ as nodes with edges $u\to ua$. A
maximal prefix code is a **frontier**: an antichain meeting every root-to-leaf
path exactly once. Longest-prefix match, insertion, deletion and prefix-range
enumeration cost $O(|w|)$ plus output; a sorted array of words with binary search
achieves the same up to a logarithmic factor, since the descendants of $u$ form
the contiguous block of words with prefix $u$.

### 3.5 Transducers

A **finite transducer** is an automaton with output words on transitions; the
relations it realises are **rational**, and a rational relation that is a partial
function is a **rational function**. It is **sequential** if the input automaton
is deterministic and output is emitted as input is consumed, **subsequential** if
a terminal output may be appended.

**Theorem 3.5.1 (closure) (standard).** Rational relations are closed under
composition, inverse and reversal; sequential functions are **not** closed under
reversal. [Berstel 1979; Sakarovitch 2009.]

**Theorem 3.5.2 (Choffrut) (standard).** A rational function is subsequential iff
it has bounded variation, equivalently the twinning property. [Choffrut 1977.]

Call $f$ **right-sequential** if $f^{R}(w)=f(w^{R})^{R}$ is sequential; such $f$
is rational but need not be sequential. The gap between "finite-state" and
"single left-to-right pass" is exactly lookahead.

### 3.6 Morphisms on words

A letter map $\pi:\Sigma\to\Gamma$ extends uniquely to $\pi^{*}$ (Thm 2.4.1);
when letter-to-letter it is **alphabetic**, length-preserving, and sequential.

## 4. Trees

### 4.1–4.2 Ordered labelled trees

An **ordered labelled tree** is $T=(V,E,r,\lambda,\sigma)$ with labels in
$\Lambda$ and a total order on each child set. Arity is unbounded: the setting is
**unranked**, and the natural recursion alternates trees and **hedges** (finite
sequences of trees).

### 4.3 The ancestor order

$u\leq_T v$ iff $u$ is on the root path to $v$.

**Proposition 4.3.1 (elementary).** $(V,\leq_T)$ is a forest order (§1.6), meets
are lowest common ancestors, and $\downarrow\!\{v\}$ is a chain of length
$\mathrm{depth}(v)+1$. $\square$

Combining 4.3.1 with §1.4 and §3.4: antichains in a tree are prefix codes over
its address alphabet, and the whole of §1.4–1.5 applies to them.

### 4.4 Tree automata

A **bottom-up tree automaton** assigns states as a function of label and child
states; for unranked trees an auxiliary word automaton reads the child-state
sequence, giving a **hedge automaton**. Regular unranked tree languages are
closed under Boolean operations with decidable emptiness. [Comon et al., *TATA*;
Brüggemann-Klein and Wood.] A bottom-up automaton is exactly a finite-carrier
algebra for the tree functor of §5.

### 4.5 Tree edit distance

The minimum-cost sequence of insertions, deletions, relabellings; a metric when
the elementary costs are. Computable in $O(n^{2}\log^{2}n)$ for ordered trees
[Zhang and Shasha 1989; Demaine et al. 2009]; **NP-hard for unordered** trees
[Zhang, Statman, Shasha 1992].

## 5. Universal algebra and recursion schemes

### 5.1–5.2 Functors, algebras, initiality

A functor $F$ assigns sets to sets and functions to functions. An **$F$-algebra**
is $\phi:F(A)\to A$; a homomorphism $(A,\phi)\to(B,\psi)$ is $f$ with
$f\circ\phi=\psi\circ F(f)$. $(\mu F,\mathrm{in})$ is **initial** if there is a
unique homomorphism from it to every algebra.

**Theorem 5.2.1 (Lambek) (standard).** The structure map of an initial algebra is
an isomorphism — the datatype is exactly its constructors.

### 5.3 Catamorphisms

The unique homomorphism $(\!(\phi)\!):\mu F\to A$ is the **catamorphism**.

**Proposition 5.3.1 (elementary).** For $F(X)=\Lambda\times X^{*}$, $(\!(\phi)\!)$
is the unique $S$ with
$$S(v)=\phi\bigl(\lambda(v),\ \langle S(c)\rangle_{c\in\mathrm{ch}(v)}\bigr),$$
computable by one post-order traversal in time linear in tree size when $\phi$ is
$O(1)$ per child. $\square$

Two consequences: the aggregate is fully determined by $\phi$; and $S(v)$ depends
only on $v$'s subtree — locality is a consequence of the recursion's shape, not an
assumption.

### 5.4 Fusion

**Theorem 5.4.1 (standard).** If $f\circ\phi=\psi\circ F(f)$ then
$f\circ(\!(\phi)\!)=(\!(\psi)\!)$. Post-processing can be absorbed into the fold.

### 5.5 Zippers and derivatives

**Theorem 5.5.1 (Huet; McBride) (standard).** The type of one-hole contexts of a
polynomial functor is its formal derivative $\partial F$ (Leibniz rule on
products, sum rule on sums). A **zipper** is a pair (focus, context). [Huet 1997;
McBride 2001.]

A *located* modification — a change plus where it occurred — is an element of
$\partial F$ at a focus.

## 6. Incremental computation

**Definition 6.1 (change structure) (Cai et al.).** A set $\Delta X$ of changes
with $\oplus:X\times\Delta X\to X$ and $\ominus$ satisfying
$x\oplus(y\ominus x)=y$. A **derivative** of $f$ is $f'$ with
$f(x\oplus dx)=f(x)\oplus f'(x,dx)$. [POPL 2014.] Changes compose, so $\Delta X$
acts on $X$ as a monoid. Correctness is the displayed equation; *usefulness* —
that $f'$ is cheaper than recomputation — is a separate question the definition
does not settle.

**6.2 Self-adjusting computation.** Record the dependency graph and propagate
[Acar 2005]. For a catamorphism the dependency graph is the tree itself.

**6.3 View maintenance.** Insertions need a monoid; deletions need inverses
(§2.7–2.8). Aggregates that are homomorphic images of a free commutative monoid
(counts, sums) maintain in both directions; idempotent ones (max, union) do not.
[Gupta and Mumick 1995; McSherry et al. 2013.]

**6.4 Memoisation, hash-consing.** Hash-consing makes structurally equal values
share representation, so equality is pointer comparison and unchanged subtrees
are recognised in $O(1)$. [Filliâtre and Conchon 2006.]

## 7. Hashing and similarity

**7.1 Collisions.** For $n$ values into $[2^{b}]$ the collision probability is
$\approx n^{2}/2^{b+1}$ (birthday bound). A **universal family** guarantees
$\Pr[h(x)=h(y)]\leq 2^{-b}$ over the choice of $h$. [Carter and Wegman 1979.]

**7.2 Merkle structures.** Digest each node from its label and its children's
digests; equality certifies subtree equality up to collision, and a change alters
digests exactly on the root path. [Merkle 1987.] Whether the child combination is
commutative decides sensitivity to sibling order.

**7.3 Rolling hashes.** $O(1)$ update under a sliding window [Karp and Rabin
1987]; content-defined boundaries make block identity insertion-stable
[Tridgell 1999; Muthitacharoen et al. 2001].

**7.4 Similarity-preserving hashing.** MinHash: $\Pr[h_\pi(A)=h_\pi(B)]=J(A,B)$
[Broder 1997]. SimHash: sign agreement probability $1-\theta/\pi$ [Charikar
2002]. LSH banding gives an S-curve $1-(1-s^{r})^{b}$ [Indyk and Motwani 1998;
Manku et al. 2007]. All estimate a **symmetric** similarity; by Remark 1.2.1 none
supplies a direction.

## 8. Metrics and comparison

**8.1 Metrics.** $d$ is a metric if it separates points, is symmetric, and
satisfies the triangle inequality.

**Proposition 8.1.1 (elementary).** $|A\triangle B|$ is a metric on finite sets.
*Proof.* $A\triangle C\subseteq(A\triangle B)\cup(B\triangle C)$. $\square$

**Proposition 8.1.2 (elementary).** A metric pulled back along an injection is a
metric. $\square$

Together: to metrise an unfamiliar object, embed it injectively into finite sets.

**8.2 Jaccard.** $J(A,B)=|A\cap B|/|A\cup B|$; $1-J$ is a metric
[Levandowsky and Winter 1971].

**8.3 Precision and recall.** $|R\cap P|/|R|$ and $|R\cap P|/|P|$. Neither is a
metric; they measure omission and commission, which are different failures.

**8.4 Entropy and partitions.** $H$, $H(\cdot\mid\cdot)$, $I(\cdot;\cdot)$ as
usual on the joint block distribution.

**8.5 Variation of information.** $\mathrm{VI}=H(X\mid Y)+H(Y\mid X)$ is a metric
on partitions [Meilă 2003, 2007]; its two summands separately distinguish
refinement from coarsening. The **adjusted Rand index** is chance-corrected but
not a metric [Hubert and Arabie 1985].

**8.6 Hierarchies.** Nested families are not partitions; §8.5 does not apply
directly. Available: tree edit distance (§4.5), Dasgupta's objective [2016], or
the §1.4 route of representing the family as a set.

## 9. Vector-space retrieval

**9.1** Documents as vectors over terms; cosine similarity [Salton et al. 1975].

**9.2** $\mathrm{idf}(t)=\log(N/df(t))$ [Spärck Jones 1972].

**9.3** Raw term-frequency cosine favours short documents; pivoted length
normalisation corrects the length-dependent gap between retrieval and relevance
probability [Singhal et al. 1996]; BM25 combines saturating term frequency with a
length term parameterised by $k_1$ and $b$ [Robertson et al. 1994].

**9.4** An **inverted index** maps terms to postings; scoring decomposes as a sum
over query terms. Incremental construction is typically log-structured [Zobel and
Moffat 2006].

**9.5** Signals on different scales are not comparable; rescaling each over its
own observed distribution precedes any combination, and the combiner is a
separate modelling choice.

# Part II — Data primals and the structures they carry

Each section names a primitive datum from `CONTENT_EXTRACTION_RULES.md`, then
identifies the least structure it carries. Nothing is assumed beyond Part I.

## 10. The detection: the atom of meaning

**Definition 10.1.** A **detection** is the 6-tuple
$$\delta = (\mathrm{xpath},\ \mathrm{category},\ \mathrm{subcategory},\ \mathrm{value},\ \mathrm{source\_attr},\ \mathrm{sub})$$
and the **detection space** is
$$\mathcal{D} \;=\; \mathcal{A} \times K \times \mathrm{Str} \times (\mathrm{Attr}\cup\{\varepsilon\}) \times \mathrm{Sub},$$
where $\mathcal{A}$ is the address monoid (§11), $K$ the 18-element set of
category-subcategory pairs, $\mathrm{Str}$ the value strings, $\mathrm{Attr}$
the attribute names of the rules document, and $\mathrm{Sub}$ the
**sub-accessor** paths of Definition 10.1.1.

**Definition 10.1.1 (sub-accessor).** An accessor may carry an ordered
sequence of values rather than one: `srcset` is a comma-separated candidate
list, a `<style>` body holds many `url(...)`, an `ld+json` script holds an
entire JSON document. The **sub-accessor** $\mathrm{sub}$ names the position
of a value *within* its accessor, and is required to be a function of the
accessor's own content — a candidate index, a character offset, an RFC 6901
JSON pointer. $\varepsilon$ when the accessor carries a single value.

The intrinsicness requirement is not decoration. An extrinsic alternative — a
counter over emissions — would make $\mathrm{sub}$ a function of the
extractor's execution order rather than of the document, so a reordering of
the detector would re-key every stored detection, and by Corollary 10.9 that
order would leak into any output exposing it.

$K$ carries no order and no operation: it is a **finite set of labels**, a
five-block partition of eighteen elements. This is the entire semantic vocabulary
of the system. Every later notion of meaning is a function of $\mathcal{D}$, and
$K$'s discreteness means the only comparisons available on kinds are equality and
membership.

**Proposition 10.2 (elementary).** A page's extraction is an element of
$\mathbb{N}^{(\mathcal{D})}$ — a finite multiset of detections, not a set.

*Proof.* Two distinct nodes may yield identical detections in every component
except the address, and one node may yield several detections differing only
in `source_attr` or in `sub`. Multiplicity is therefore possible and carries
information. $\square$

That the extraction is a multiset rather than a set is the first structural fact
of the architecture, and it makes §14 available immediately.

### 10.2.1 The address is a key

Write $\pi:\mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{K}$ for the projection forgetting the value,
$$\pi(\delta) = (\mathrm{xpath},\ \mathrm{category},\ \mathrm{subcategory},\ \mathrm{source\_attr},\ \mathrm{sub}),$$
and call $\pi(\delta)$ the **address** of $\delta$.

**Proposition 10.2.1.** $\pi$ is injective on a page's extraction: no two
detections of one page share an address and differ in value.

*Proof.* Fix an address. Its first component determines a node (Prop. 11.2),
its fourth an accessor of that node, and its fifth a position within that
accessor's content. A node, an accessor and a position within it jointly
determine at most one value, since the accessor's content is a single string
and the position selects into it. $\square$

**Remark 10.2.2 (what the proposition rests on).** The argument needs
$\mathrm{Sub}$ to be present *and* intrinsic. Without it, the fourth component
alone would have to determine the value, which is false wherever an accessor
carries a sequence — and it is a claim of exactly that form that an earlier
revision used, so this is the load-bearing step and not a technicality.
Measured over the 54-page corpus with $\mathrm{Sub}$ removed, three accessor
kinds break it on real pages; with it, 106,789 detections yield 106,098
addresses and **none** carries two values (`scripts/audit_addressing.py`).

**Corollary 10.2.3.** The extraction's multiplicity lies entirely in
$\ker\pi$ being trivial on values: it is a multiset over $\mathcal{D}$, but a
*set* when keyed by address. So an address is admissible as a primary key,
and — being a function of position and provenance rather than of payload — it
does not degenerate where the payload is absent, which by Corollary 22.5.11 is
exactly where a content-derived key does. $\square$

**Remark 10.3 (provenance is a primal).** `source_attr` is the only component
recording *how* a value was observed. By Remark 1.2.1, any operation that must
choose between two values carrying the same string can only do so using data
outside the string — and `source_attr` is the only such data present. Its
presence in the primal is therefore not redundancy.

### 10.4 Detection and observation are different alphabets

§0 grounds meaning in detectability. That is right, and it is not the same as
grounding it in *observability*, which is what an equality between system states
requires. The two must be separated.

**Definition 10.4.** The **detection alphabet** is the set of facts an extractor
can determine about a document — for us the categories and vocabularies of
`CONTENT_EXTRACTION_RULES.md`. The **observation alphabet** is the set of
functions on *system state* realised by the system's public output surfaces.

**Proposition 10.5 (elementary).** The two alphabets are in general
incomparable.

*Proof.* A fact may be detected and never exposed — an internal field consumed
only by later stages — so detection does not imply observation. A fact may be
exposed and never detected — a retrieval score, a row count, a timestamp — so
observation does not imply detection. Neither inclusion holds. $\square$

**Corollary 10.6.** Observational equivalence of system states is defined
relative to the *observation* alphabet. Grounding it in the detection alphabet
is a category error: the detection alphabet describes what can be found in a
page, not what can be distinguished about a stored state.

The distinction matters most for facts that are *properties of an instance
rather than of content* — its identity, its position, how many times it occurred.
None of these is a content category, so none appears in a detection alphabet; all
of them may or may not be exposed, and whether they are decides which states are
equal.

**Corollary 10.7 (observability is not free).** The observation alphabet is a
property of the output surfaces, hence enumerable by inspecting them and
changeable by adding or removing one. Enlarging it refines observational
equivalence, so states previously equal may become distinguishable, and an
equality that held may fail.

*Proof.* Equivalence is the intersection of the kernels of the alphabet's
functions; adding a function can only refine it. $\square$

Corollary 10.7 has a consequence worth stating in plain terms: **adding an output
is a semantic change, not merely a feature.** A single field printed by a single
command enlarges what an observer can distinguish, and can thereby invalidate an
invariant stated up to observational equivalence — including, as §22.5.8 shows,
the compatibility of an identity scheme's requirements.

### 10.8 Enforcing an equivalence rather than asserting it

Corollary 10.7 describes how an alphabet grows. The construction below is its
dual, and it is the only way to make a claimed equivalence hold by construction.

**Proposition 10.8 (elementary).** Let $o : C \to V$ be an output and $\sim$ an
equivalence on $C$. Then $o$ cannot distinguish $\sim$-equivalent states **iff**
$o$ factors through the quotient, i.e. $o = \bar{o} \circ q$ for some
$\bar{o} : C/{\sim} \to V$ and $q$ the quotient map.

*Proof.* If $o$ factors then $c \sim c'$ gives $q(c) = q(c')$ hence
$o(c) = o(c')$. Conversely if $o$ is constant on classes, $\bar{o}([c]) = o(c)$
is well defined and $o = \bar{o} \circ q$. $\square$

The proposition is trivial and the discipline it names is not. An equivalence
that a design *asserts* — "row order is not observable" — is a claim about every
output, and stays true only while no output reads the distinguishing data. An
equivalence that outputs *factor through* is a property of their construction and
cannot be violated by an output that respects the factorisation.

The practical form is a rule about **incidental order and incidental
representation**: wherever an output must impose an order that the data does not
determine — ties in a ranking, iteration over a hash table, enumeration of a set
— the resolution must be a function of the data, not of the storage. Otherwise
the storage layout leaks through the tie, and the equivalence quietly fails on
exactly the inputs where two things are equally good.

**Corollary 10.9.** An output whose ordering is resolved by storage position
makes storage position observable, and hence refines observational equivalence by
at least the orbit of the storage permutation. $\square$

Corollary 10.9 is worth stating separately because such an output does not look
like an output of position. It reports scores and identifiers, never an index —
yet the *order* in which it reports them is a function of the index, and order is
observable.

### 10.10 Testing invariance, and a generator argument that does not apply

Layout-invariance is a claim quantified over a group of permutations, so testing
it invites an efficiency: adjacent transpositions generate $S_n$, so checking
$n-1$ of them ought to suffice for all $n!$.

**Proposition 10.10 (elementary).** Let a group $G$ act on $C$ and let $S$
generate $G$. If $f(\sigma c) = f(c)$ **for every $c \in C$** and every
$\sigma \in S$, then $f(\sigma c) = f(c)$ for every $\sigma \in G$.

*Proof.* Write $\sigma = \sigma_1 \cdots \sigma_k$ with $\sigma_i \in S$ and
induct: $f(\sigma_1(\sigma_2\cdots\sigma_k c)) = f(\sigma_2\cdots\sigma_k c)$ by
the hypothesis at the state $\sigma_2\cdots\sigma_k c$. $\square$

**The hypothesis is universally quantified over states, and testing is not.**
Observing $f(\sigma_i c_0) = f(c_0)$ at a single baseline $c_0$ establishes
nothing about $f(\sigma_1 (\sigma_2 c_0))$, because $\sigma_2 c_0$ was never
tested. The induction has no base to stand on beyond one state, and the
efficiency is unavailable.

This is not a technicality to be waved away. Measured on our own system: all
$n-1$ adjacent transpositions of a baseline passed, while random permutations
of the same baseline exposed a genuine dependence on layout. A generator-only
audit would have reported invariance that does not hold.

### 10.11 Precision as an observation channel

**Proposition 10.11 (elementary).** Floating-point addition is commutative and
has an identity, but is **not associative**. Hence $(\mathbb{F}, \oplus, 0)$ is a
commutative magma with identity and *not* a monoid, and the universal property of
the free commutative monoid (Thm 2.4.1) does not apply to it.

*Proof.* Associativity fails on standard binary formats: with $\epsilon$ the
unit roundoff, $(1 \oplus \epsilon) \oplus \epsilon = 1$ while
$1 \oplus (\epsilon \oplus \epsilon) > 1$. $\square$

**Corollary 10.12.** A floating-point aggregate over a collection is a function
of the *sequence* in which the collection is enumerated, not of the collection.
Consequently an aggregate over stored rows depends on row order, and reporting it
at full precision makes row order observable — by COR-10.9, without the output
ever mentioning an index. $\square$

**Corollary 10.13.** An output reporting a floating-point quantity to greater
precision than the computation is stable to does not factor through the layout
quotient. Rounding to a tolerance exceeding the layout-induced spread restores
the factorisation (PROP-10.8), at the cost of discarding digits that carried no
information. $\square$

The scope of 10.11 is worth marking: it does **not** disturb the integer-valued
aggregates of §14, which are exact. It disturbs every real-valued one — scores,
norms, similarities — and with them any claim that such a quantity is a
homomorphism out of a free commutative monoid. Where this document treats
aggregates as monoid homomorphisms, the treatment is sound for the exact carriers
and is an idealisation for the floating-point ones.

## 11. Addresses

**Definition 11.1.** Let $\Lambda$ be the element labels and
$\Sigma_{\mathcal A}=\Lambda\times\mathbb{N}_{\geq1}$ the **address alphabet**, a
letter being a label with a sibling index. The **address monoid** is
$\mathcal{A}=\Sigma_{\mathcal A}^{*}$, free by §2.4. An absolute xpath is an
element of $\mathcal{A}$.

**Proposition 11.2 (elementary).** The address map $\alpha$ is injective, and $u$
is an ancestor of $v$ iff $\alpha(u)\prec\alpha(v)$.

*Proof.* Each letter determines a node uniquely among its siblings; induct on
depth. Ancestry is prefix by construction. $\square$

**Corollary 11.3.** The addresses of a document form a **trie** (§3.4); ancestry
is a prefix test; the ancestor order is a forest order (§1.6, §4.3.1); and any
family of pairwise non-nested addresses is a **prefix code**, so Kraft's
inequality (Thm 3.4.1) constrains it. $\square$

**Proposition 11.3.1 (sibling order is not recoverable).** Document order on
the children of a node is not a function of their addresses.

*Proof.* The alphabet $\Sigma_{\mathcal A}=\Lambda\times\mathbb{N}_{\geq1}$
indexes a node among its siblings *of the same label*, so the letters
$(\texttt{head},1)$ and $(\texttt{body},1)$ are both first-of-label and carry
nothing distinguishing their order. Take two documents identical except that
one has `head` before `body` and the other the reverse: their address sets are
equal while their document orders differ, so no function of the addresses
computes the order. $\square$

**Remark 11.3.2.** This bounds what Corollary 11.3 delivers, and the bound is
easy to miss because so much *is* recoverable. Ancestry survives the encoding
because it is prefix; the order of same-label siblings survives because the
index carries it; document order across labels does not survive at all. An
injective encoding of a structure need not encode every order on that
structure, and no amount of work on the encoded form recovers what the
encoding dropped.

The consequence for an implementation is that document order has to be
recorded during the walk that produced the addresses, as a separate datum. Two
substitutes that look adequate are not: ordering by label is simply wrong
($\texttt{body}$ precedes $\texttt{head}$ alphabetically), and ordering by the
arrival of *detections* is wrong because detections are sparse — an element
with no attributes and no direct text yields none, so a container inherits the
position of whichever descendant was reported first. Measured, the latter
mis-ordered 15.9% of multi-label parents; the walk position mis-orders none.

**Definition 11.4 (shadow boundary).** The shadow segment of
`CONTENT_EXTRACTION_RULES.md` §8.4 is one further letter of
$\Sigma_{\mathcal A}$. Consequently shadow content lies in the *same* monoid
$\mathcal{A}$, and 11.2–11.3 apply to it verbatim. This is a structural
statement, not a convention: no separate theory of shadow addressing is needed
because the alphabet absorbs the boundary.

## 12. Patterns

**Definition 12.1.** Let $\pi:\Sigma_{\mathcal A}\to\Lambda$ erase the index. By
§3.6 it extends to an alphabetic morphism $\pi^{*}:\mathcal{A}\to\Lambda^{*}$.
The **pattern monoid** is $\mathcal{G}=\Lambda^{*}$, and a generalization map is
any $g:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{G}$ agreeing with $\pi^{*}$ below a designated
anchor and with the identity above it.

**Proposition 12.2 (elementary).** $\pi^{*}$ is a surjective monoid homomorphism
and is length-preserving; its kernel congruence is "same labels in the same
order, indices arbitrary". $\square$

**Proposition 12.3 (elementary).** Any $g$ of Definition 12.1 induces an
equivalence $\sim_g$ on nodes, hence a quotient $V/{\sim_g}$; and the
**commutation count** $|[v]_{\sim_g}|$ is well defined on it. $\square$

**Proposition 12.4 (elementary).** If $g$ retains a nonempty verbatim prefix that
is recoverable from the output — because index-bearing letters cannot occur in
$\mathrm{im}(\pi^{*})$ — then nodes with distinct anchors are never
$\sim_g$-equivalent.

*Proof.* The boundary between the verbatim and erased segments is detectable in
the image, so the prefix is a function of $g(v)$; distinct prefixes give distinct
values. $\square$

**Proposition 12.5 (directionality).** A generalization anchored at the *deepest*
qualifying ancestor is a rational function that is right-sequential but in
general not sequential.

*Proof.* Right-to-left a single bit suffices: erase indices until the first
anchor letter, then copy. Hence the reversal is sequential and $g$ is rational
(Thm 3.5.1). For the negative direction, the output attributable to a prefix
depends on whether an anchor occurs later; a sequential transducer emits that
output having read only the prefix, and the two continuations are
distinguishable, so no finite state set suffices (Prop. 3.7 pattern; Thm 3.5.2
gives the exact criterion). $\square$

The content: "computable by a finite-state device" and "computable in one
left-to-right pass over a path" differ here, and the difference is lookahead.

## 13. Values and tokens

**Definition 13.1.** A **value** is an element of $\mathrm{Str}=\Sigma^{*}$ for a
character alphabet $\Sigma$. A **tokenization** is a map
$\theta:\mathrm{Str}\to\mathbb{N}^{(\Sigma_{\mathrm{tok}})}$ into multisets of
tokens.

**Proposition 13.2 (elementary).** $\theta$ is not injective and is not a monoid
homomorphism for concatenation: it forgets order, multiplicity boundaries, and
all characters removed by folding. $\square$

Everything downstream of tokenization is therefore a statement about
$\mathbb{N}^{(\Sigma_{\mathrm{tok}})}$, not about $\mathrm{Str}$, and no property
of a value survives that is not a property of its multiset.

**Proposition 13.3 (elementary).** If $\theta$ emits $n$-grams for $n \geq 2$
alongside unigrams, then $\mathrm{im}(\theta) \subsetneq
\mathbb{N}^{(\Sigma_{\mathrm{tok}})}$: the image is a proper subset, and the
coordinates of a vector in it are not independent.

*Proof.* Let $\Sigma_{\mathrm{tok}}$ contain both unigrams and bigrams, and let
$m$ have $m(ab) > 0$ for a bigram $ab$. Any text tokenizing to $m$ contains the
token $a$ followed by $b$, so $m(a) > 0$ and $m(b) > 0$. The vector assigning
$1$ to $ab$ and $0$ to both $a$ and $b$ therefore lies outside the image while
lying in the carrier. $\square$

Call the image the **coherent** vectors, $\mathcal{K} = \mathrm{im}(\theta)$.
Coherence in the sense of the proof — every $n$-gram entails its constituents —
is *necessary*; a counting identity relating $n$-gram and unigram totals is a
further necessary condition; whether the two together are *sufficient* is open.

The consequence is that any statement quantified over
$\mathbb{N}^{(\Sigma_{\mathrm{tok}})}$ is quantified over states no value can
produce. Where the quantification matters — in particular where a construction
is claimed to realise an arbitrary frequency vector — it must be restricted to
$\mathcal{K}$.

## 14. Counts and frequency vectors

**Definition 14.1.** A **frequency vector** is an element of
$\mathbb{N}^{(\Sigma_{\mathrm{tok}})}$: finitely supported, integer-valued.

Three structures coexist on this carrier, and they are independent:

1. the **free commutative monoid** $(\mathbb{N}^{(\Sigma)},\uplus,0)$ — §2.6;
2. the **distributive lattice** $(\mathbb{N}^{(\Sigma)},\subseteq_m)$ —
   Prop. 2.6.1;
3. the **embedding into a real vector space** $\mathbb{N}^{(\Sigma)}
   \hookrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}$, which supplies the inner product, norms
   and cosine of §9.1.

**Proposition 14.2 (elementary).** $(\mathbb{N}^{(\Sigma)},\uplus)$ is
cancellative; hence by Thm 2.8.1 it embeds in $\mathrm{Gr}=\mathbb{Z}^{(\Sigma)}$
and every $\uplus$-aggregate over it admits exact removal. $\square$

**Proposition 14.3 (elementary).** Document frequency is a monoid homomorphism
$$df:\ \mathbb{N}^{(\text{documents})}\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{(\Sigma)},
\qquad d\mapsto \mathbf{1}_{\mathrm{supp}(\theta(d))},$$
determined by its values on single documents (Thm 2.4.1 in the commutative
setting). $\square$

**Proposition 14.4 (elementary).** An aggregate valued in an idempotent monoid —
$(\mathbb{N},\max)$, $(\mathcal{P}(X),\cup)$ — is non-cancellative, hence embeds
in no group, hence admits no exact removal from the aggregate alone.

*Proof.* §2.7 gives the counterexample; Thm 2.8.1 gives the non-embedding; and
the aggregate value is by hypothesis the only retained state. $\square$

Propositions 14.2 and 14.4 are the same theorem applied to two aggregates, and
they differ in conclusion. Which of the two a given statistic falls under is
decided entirely by its operation, not by its type.

**Remark 14.5.** $\mathrm{idf}$ is not an aggregate in this sense: it is a
function of $df$ *and* of $|\mathcal{D}|$, so it is not determined by any
per-document homomorphism. Structurally it is a *derived quantity*, and §23 is
where that distinction has consequences.

**Proposition 14.6 (elementary).** $df$ is determined by the corpus: for
$\mathcal{D} \in \mathbb{N}^{(\text{documents})}$,
$$df_{\mathcal{D}}[t] \;=\; \bigl|\{\, d \in \mathcal{D} \;:\; t \in \mathrm{supp}(\theta(d)) \,\}\bigr|.$$
Hence a state carrying $\mathcal{D}$ and $df$ as independent components is
*redundant*, and admits states in which the two disagree.

*Proof.* The displayed formula evaluates $df$ from $\mathcal{D}$ alone, so $df$
is a function of $\mathcal{D}$; a pair $(\mathcal{D}, df)$ with $df \neq
df_{\mathcal{D}}$ is expressible but denotes no corpus. $\square$

The practical reading is a constraint on any *representation* of a corpus: if
$df$ is materialised for speed, its agreement with the corpus is an invariant to
be maintained and checked, never an assumption. It cannot be inferred from the
type.

**Proposition 14.7 (elementary).** Let observations of a corpus include the
*identity* of the document a result came from. Then a multiset
$\mathcal{D} \in \mathbb{N}^{(\text{documents})}$ does not determine the
observations, and the carrier must be an **indexed family**
$a : I \rightharpoonup_{\mathrm{fin}} \mathbb{N}^{(\Sigma)}$ over an identifier
set $I$.

*Proof.* Let $x \neq y$ and $i \neq j$. The assignments
$\{i \mapsto x,\ j \mapsto y\}$ and $\{i \mapsto y,\ j \mapsto x\}$ are distinct
indexed families with the same underlying multiset $\{\!\{x, y\}\!\}$, while an
observation returning the identifier for a query matching $x$ yields $i$ on the
first and $j$ on the second. So the multiset does not determine the observation,
and any abstraction to it is not observationally faithful. $\square$

Whether the multiset or the indexed family is the right carrier is therefore not
a matter of taste: it is settled by the observation alphabet. Under retrieval
that returns only *scores*, the multiset suffices; under retrieval that returns
*which document matched*, it does not. §10.2's multiset of detections and this
proposition are consistent — detections are observed in aggregate, whereas
retrieval results are observed by name.

## 15. Fields, records, JSON

**Definition 15.1.** A **field** is a pair (name, value); a **record** is a finite
partial function $\mathrm{Str}\rightharpoonup_{\mathrm{fin}} X$. Write
$\mathcal{R}(X)$ for records with values in $X$.

**Proposition 15.2 (elementary).** $\mathcal{R}(X)$ under union is a **partial**
monoid only: union is defined when the domains agree on their overlap, and
otherwise requires a conflict policy. Under a left-biased or right-biased policy
it becomes a monoid, non-commutative and idempotent. $\square$

By §14.4 a biased-union record aggregate is not retractable. This is a property
of records as such, before any choice about our data.

**Definition 15.3.** The JSON values are the initial algebra
$$\mathcal{J}=\mu X.\ \bigl(1+\mathbb{B}+\mathbb{R}+\mathrm{Str}+X^{*}+\mathcal{R}(X)\bigr),$$
a polynomial functor: null, booleans, numbers, strings, arrays, objects.

**Corollary 15.4.** $\mathcal{J}$ has an initial-algebra structure, so
(i) Lambek (Thm 5.2.1) applies — a JSON value is exactly its constructors;
(ii) every total function out of $\mathcal{J}$ defined by structural recursion is
a catamorphism (§5.3), hence unique and computable in one post-order pass;
(iii) fusion (Thm 5.4.1) applies to post-processing. $\square$

**Remark 15.5.** By `CONTENT_EXTRACTION_RULES.md` §7, parsed JSON is flattened to
leaf paths and each leaf emitted as a detection with `source_attr` of the form
`ld_json:key`. Structurally this is a map $\mathcal{J}\to\mathbb{N}^{(\mathcal D)}$
— embedded JSON enters the *same* detection multiset as markup content and is
not a separate sort. Consequently no result in Part III needs a JSON case.

## 16. Nodes and content trees

**Definition 16.1.** $\mathcal{T}=\mu X.\ \Lambda\times X^{*}$, the unranked
ordered labelled trees of §4.2; a **content tree** is an element of $\mathcal{T}$
whose labels carry the detections of §10 at each node.

**Proposition 16.2.** Every extraction quantity defined per-node and combined
associatively over children is a catamorphism $(\!(\phi)\!)$, hence unique,
computable in one post-order pass, and dependent at $v$ only on $v$'s subtree
(Prop. 5.3.1). $\square$

This is the strongest available statement about the extraction stage, and it
follows from `CONTENT_EXTRACTION_RULES.md` §9.6 — *detection is per-node and
context-free* — with no further hypothesis. The rules document's context-freedom
is precisely the condition that makes $\phi$ well defined.

**Proposition 16.3 (elementary).** $\mathcal{T}$ and $\mathcal{J}$ are initial
algebras of different functors, and neither is a retract of the other: $\mathcal{J}$
has scalar leaves and keyed children, $\mathcal{T}$ has labelled nodes and ordered
children. A map between them is therefore genuine data, not a coercion. $\square$

## 17. Markdown lines

**Definition 17.1.** A **markdown line** is a pair $(k,p)\in\mathbb{N}\times
\mathrm{Str}$: an indentation depth and a payload. $\mathcal{M}=(\mathbb{N}\times
\mathrm{Str})^{*}$ is the set of line sequences, free by §2.4.

**Definition 17.2.** A depth sequence $(k_1,\dots,k_n)$ is **well-formed** if
$k_1=0$ and $k_{i+1}\leq k_i+1$.

**Theorem 17.3 (elementary; classical bijection).** Well-formed depth sequences of
length $n$ are in bijection with ordered forests on $n$ nodes, the correspondence
sending a node to its depth in pre-order.

*Proof.* Pre-order traversal of a forest yields $k_1=0$ and increases depth by at
most one per step, since a node's depth exceeds its predecessor's by one exactly
when it is that predecessor's first child, and is otherwise no greater.
Conversely, given a well-formed sequence, attach the $i$-th node to the most
recent node of depth $k_i-1$; this is well defined because depth increases by at
most one, and inverts the traversal. $\square$

**Corollary 17.4.** Indentation is a **complete** encoding of ordered tree
structure: no bracketing is required, and the encoding is bijective onto
well-formed sequences. The number of such sequences on $n$ nodes is the $n$-th
Catalan number. $\square$

**Proposition 17.5 (faithfulness).** The printing map $\mathcal{T}\to\mathcal{M}$
is injective iff payloads are drawn from a sublanguage containing neither the
line separator nor a leading indentation character.

*Proof.* If a payload contains a separator it produces two lines whose second is
indistinguishable from a genuine node; the fibre over the printed sequence then
contains at least two trees. If payloads avoid the delimiter alphabet, the line
decomposition and the depth of each line are recoverable, and Thm 17.3 recovers
the structure. $\square$

Proposition 17.5 identifies the exact precondition under which a markdown
rendering loses nothing: it is a condition on the *value alphabet*, not on the
tree.

# Part III — Interactions between the structures

## 18. The carrier diagram

Part II gives five carriers: $\mathcal{A}$ (addresses, free monoid),
$\mathcal{G}$ (patterns, free monoid), $\mathcal{T}$ (content trees, initial
algebra), $\mathcal{R}/\mathcal{J}$ (records and JSON, initial algebra),
$\mathcal{M}$ (markdown, free monoid), $\mathbb{N}^{(\Sigma)}$ (frequency
vectors, free commutative monoid).

The maps between them are of exactly three kinds, and the classification is
exhaustive because Part II admits no others:

| kind | example | Part I basis |
|---|---|---|
| **homomorphism** — preserves the operation | $\pi^{*}:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{G}$; $\theta$ into $\mathbb{N}^{(\Sigma)}$; $df$ | §2.3–2.4 |
| **catamorphism** — folds an initial algebra | $\mathcal{T}\to M$; $\mathcal{J}\to M$; $\mathcal{T}\to\mathcal{M}$ | §5.3 |
| **quotient** — collapses by a congruence | $V\to V/{\sim_g}$; $\mathrm{Str}\to$ token multisets | §1.2, §12.3 |

**Proposition 18.1.** A composite of homomorphisms is a homomorphism; a
catamorphism followed by a homomorphism is a catamorphism (Thm 5.4.1); a quotient
followed by a well-defined map on classes is a map on the quotient. Hence any
composite along the diagram is again of one of the three kinds. $\square$

The diagram is therefore closed, and reasoning about a pipeline stage never
leaves this vocabulary.

## 19. Quotients, congruences, and the loss of direction

**Definition 19.1.** A **congruence** on an algebra is an equivalence compatible
with the operations, so that the quotient inherits them.

**Proposition 19.2.** $\sim_g$ (§12.3) is induced by a homomorphism on addresses,
hence is a congruence on $\mathcal{A}$; the pattern of a node is a function of
its address alone and not of its subtree. $\square$

**Proposition 19.3 (elementary).** Let $\lesssim$ be a preorder on units with
induced equivalence $\equiv$. Then:
(i) $\max_{\lesssim}$ is well defined on $X/{\equiv}$ and its value is an
antichain of the quotient poset (§1.3);
(ii) choosing a representative within a class is *independent* data, not
determined by $\lesssim$;
(iii) if $\lesssim$ is replaced by any equivalence, (i) degenerates — every class
is maximal — and all discriminating power is lost.

*Proof.* (i) is §1.2 plus §1.3. (ii) holds because $\lesssim$ relates all members
of a class in both directions, so it distinguishes none. (iii) is immediate: an
equivalence has no strict part. $\square$

Proposition 19.3 is the abstract form of Remark 1.2.1 and of §7.4: **a hash gives
$\equiv$; only an order gives $\lesssim$; and the representative within a class
requires a third datum**, which by Remark 10.3 can only be `source_attr`. The
three-part structure — order, quotient, representative — is forced by the algebra,
not chosen.

## 20. Linearization: printing and parsing

**Definition 20.1.** A **linearization** is a map $L:\mathcal{T}\to\mathcal{M}$;
a **parser** is $P:\mathcal{M}\rightharpoonup\mathcal{T}$.

**Definition 20.2.** $L$ is **faithful** if $P\circ L=\mathrm{id}_{\mathcal T}$,
and **canonical** if $L\circ P=\mathrm{id}$ on $\mathrm{dom}(P)$.

**Proposition 20.3.** Under the hypothesis of Prop. 17.5, indentation
linearization is faithful; it is canonical exactly on the well-formed sequences
of Def. 17.2, and Thm 17.3 identifies $\mathrm{im}(L)$ with that set. $\square$

**Proposition 20.4.** $L$ is a catamorphism into the free monoid $\mathcal{M}$.

*Proof.* Printing a node emits its own line then concatenates its children's
printings with depth incremented; concatenation is associative with identity, so
the combination is a monoid operation and §5.3 applies. $\square$

**Corollary 20.5.** By fusion (Thm 5.4.1), any homomorphic post-processing of the
printed text may be absorbed into the print fold; and by Prop. 20.3 the round trip
is lossless only within the payload sublanguage of Prop. 17.5. $\square$

**Remark 20.6.** Faithfulness and canonicity are independent. Faithfulness says no
tree is lost; canonicity says no two markdown documents denote the same tree. A
linearization can have either without the other, and which is needed depends on
whether the text or the tree is the artifact of record.

## 21. Annotation: projections and fibres

This section treats the interaction that has no counterpart in Part II: a
*visible* structure carrying *hidden* data.

**Definition 21.1.** An **annotated tree** is a content tree $T\in\mathcal{T}$
together with the maps $\alpha:V\to\mathcal{A}$ and $g:V\to\mathcal{G}$ of §11–12.

**Definition 21.2.** A **distillation** is a partial surjection
$\rho:V\rightharpoonup V_{\mathrm{vis}}$ onto the nodes of a rendered tree, undefined
on nodes contributing nothing. The **fibre** over a visible node $u$ is
$\rho^{-1}(u)\subseteq V$.

**Proposition 21.3.** The accessors of a visible node are exactly the images of
its fibre:
$$\mathrm{Abs}(u)=\alpha\bigl[\rho^{-1}(u)\bigr]\subseteq\mathcal{A},
\qquad \mathrm{Gen}(u)=g\bigl[\rho^{-1}(u)\bigr]\subseteq\mathcal{G}.$$
$\mathrm{Abs}(u)$ is a singleton iff $\rho$ is injective at $u$. $\square$

**Proposition 21.4.** If $\rho$ merges only $\sim_g$-equivalent nodes then
$\mathrm{Gen}(u)$ is a singleton while $\mathrm{Abs}(u)$ need not be.

*Proof.* $g$ is constant on $\sim_g$-classes by definition; $\alpha$ is injective
(Prop. 11.2), so distinct merged nodes have distinct addresses. $\square$

Proposition 21.4 is the structural reason the two accessors are *both* required
and are not redundant: **generalized survives merging, absolute does not**. Which
one a consumer needs is determined by whether it addresses a class or an
occurrence.

**Definition 21.5.** A **section** of $\rho$ is $s:V_{\mathrm{vis}}\to V$ with
$\rho\circ s=\mathrm{id}$ — a canonical choice of source node per visible node.

**Proposition 21.6.** Sections of a surjection always exist; they are unique iff
every fibre is a singleton; and by Prop. 19.3(ii) a choice of section is
equivalent to a choice of representative in each fibre, hence is independent data
not determined by $\rho$. $\square$

**Corollary 21.7.** A rendering that merges (i.e. deduplicates) necessarily has
non-singleton fibres, hence admits no canonical section, hence the "hidden"
accessor attached to a visible line is either a *set* or a *choice*. There is no
third option. $\square$

**Remark 21.8.** In categorical terms $\rho$ is a map in the slice over
$V_{\mathrm{vis}}$ and $\mathrm{Abs},\mathrm{Gen}$ are its fibrewise images;
nothing beyond the elementary theory of surjections and fibres is used, and the
statements above are all that is needed.

## 22. Aggregation across the diagram

**Proposition 22.1.** Composing the catamorphism $\mathcal{T}\to
\mathbb{N}^{(\mathcal D)}$ (extraction, §16.2) with the homomorphism
$\mathbb{N}^{(\mathcal D)}\to\mathbb{N}^{(\Sigma)}$ (tokenization of values,
§13.1) yields a catamorphism $\mathcal{T}\to\mathbb{N}^{(\Sigma)}$.

*Proof.* Fusion, Thm 5.4.1, the second map being a monoid homomorphism. $\square$

**Corollary 22.2.** Frequency vectors are computable in the *same* traversal as
extraction, with no intermediate materialisation, and by Prop. 14.2 the result is
retractable. $\square$

**Proposition 22.3.** An aggregate is retractable iff its carrier is cancellative
(Thm 2.8.1); this property is *not* preserved by arbitrary composition — a
cancellative aggregate composed with an idempotent one is idempotent. $\square$

Retractability is therefore a property of the *whole* composite, and establishing
it for each stage separately does not establish it for the pipeline.

### 22.4 Stored redundancy and the origin of invariants

An aggregate may be *recomputed* on demand or *stored* alongside its source. The
choice is usually framed as time against space. It is also, and more
consequentially, a choice about how many invariants the representation has.

**Proposition 22.4 (elementary).** Let a representation range over the product
$C = C_1 \times \cdots \times C_n$ and suppose a component satisfies
$c_i = f(c_{-i})$ for a total $f : C_{-i} \to C_i$. Then:

1. if $|C_i| \geq 2$, the predicate $c_i = f(c_{-i})$ is a **nontrivial
   invariant**: states violating it are expressible;
2. if $c_i$ is not stored and is computed on demand, the same predicate is a
   **tautology**: no state violating it exists to be expressed.

*Proof.* (1) Fix any $c_{-i} \in C_{-i}$. Since $|C_i| \geq 2$ there is some
$c_i' \in C_i$ with $c_i' \neq f(c_{-i})$, and $(c_{-i}, c_i') \in C$ violates
the predicate. (2) With $c_i$ absent from the state, $f(c_{-i})$ is the only
value the accessor denotes, so the equation holds definitionally. $\square$

Note what the hypothesis is *not*. Constancy of $f$ is irrelevant: a constant
$f$ with $|C_i| \geq 2$ still admits violating states. What matters is only that
the component's carrier is non-trivial — that there is another value it could
wrongly hold.

**Corollary 22.5 (elementary).** Under the hypotheses of 22.4(1), the legal
states — those satisfying the predicate — are in bijection with $C_{-i}$, via
$c_{-i} \mapsto (c_{-i}, f(c_{-i}))$. Hence storing $c_i$ leaves the number of
legal states unchanged and adds exactly
$$|C_{-i}| \cdot (|C_i| - 1)$$
expressible-but-illegal states.

*Proof.* The map is injective, and its image is the graph of $f$, which is
precisely the legal set; so legal states number $|C_{-i}|$. The full product has
$|C_{-i}| \cdot |C_i|$ elements. $\square$

Corollary 22.5 is the exact form of the trade, and it is worth preferring to the
loose statement that "each redundancy adds an invariant clause". Clause *counts*
are not well defined — a single predicate may assert an arbitrary conjunction —
whereas the cardinality of the illegal region is. Storing a derived component
buys nothing in expressible legal states and pays in states that are wrong.

**Corollary 22.5.1 (detection is not prevention) (elementary).** Write
$I = \{\, c \in C : c_i \neq f(c_{-i}) \,\}$ for the illegal region. Then:

1. $I \neq \emptyset$ whenever $|C_i| \geq 2$, **independently of whether any
   predicate is evaluated**;
2. evaluating the predicate decides membership in $I$; it does not alter $I$;
3. $I = \emptyset$ if and only if $c_i$ is not stored.

*Proof.* (1) is 22.4(1). (2) A predicate is a function on $C$; its evaluation
does not change $C$. (3) Right to left is 22.4(2); left to right is the
contrapositive of (1). $\square$

The distinction is worth naming because the two remedies are routinely conflated.
Adding a check is **detection**: the wrong states remain reachable and are now
merely reportable. Removing the stored component is **prevention**: the wrong
states cease to exist. Only the second makes the property a tautology, and no
amount of checking approaches it.

**Corollary 22.5.2 (revision of the derivation) (elementary).** Suppose $f$ is
replaced by $f'$ with $f' \neq f$. Then:

1. **stored, unchecked** — states satisfying $c_i = f(c_{-i})$ but not
   $c_i = f'(c_{-i})$ persist, are illegal under $f'$, and nothing reports them;
2. **stored, checked** — such states are reported, and restoring legality
   requires recomputing $c_i$ across every stored state, i.e. a migration;
3. **derived** — the predicate is a tautology under $f'$ exactly as under $f$,
   and no state is affected.

*Proof.* Immediate from 22.4 applied to each regime. $\square$

Corollary 22.5.2 is the decisive one when $f$ is *expected* to change. A stored
derived value is a snapshot of the derivation at the moment it was taken; a
derived one tracks the derivation. Where the definition of $f$ is settled the
distinction is a performance trade; where $f$ is known to be provisional, storing
it converts every future correction into a migration, and storing it unchecked
converts the correction into silent staleness.

### 22.5.3 Provisionality propagates

**Definition 22.5.3.** A map is **provisional** when it is expected to be
replaced. A stored value is provisional when its defining map is provisional, or
when any value it depends on is — that is, provisionality is closed under the
transitive *depends-on* relation.

**Proposition 22.5.4 (blast radius) (elementary).** Replacing $f$ by $f'$
invalidates exactly those stored values whose dependency closure contains $f$,
and no others.

*Proof.* A stored value not depending on $f$ is a function of components none of
which $f$ determines, so its defining equation is unaffected. One that does
depend on $f$ has its defining equation stated in terms of $f$, and $f' \neq f$
means the equation may now fail. $\square$

The proposition is trivial and its use is not: it says the cost of a revision is
measured over the *closure*, not over the direct consumers. A single provisional
map reached through two derivations invalidates as thoroughly as one reached
directly.

**Corollary 22.5.5 (identity must not be provisional) (elementary).** Let an
identifier be $\mathrm{id} = h(\dots, p, \dots)$ with $h$ injective in $p$, and
let $p$ be provisional. Then replacing $p$'s map re-keys every state whose $p$
changes. The identifier therefore does not persist across the revision.

*Proof.* Injectivity in $p$ makes $\mathrm{id}$ differ wherever $p$ does; by
22.5.4 that is the whole closure. $\square$

An identifier exists to be stable under everything except the identity of what it
names. Corollary 22.5.5 says that if its definition reaches a provisional map,
that guarantee fails exactly when the system is corrected — the moment at which
continuity of identity is most needed, since a correction is precisely when one
wants to know that the thing before and the thing after are the same thing.

The practical rule this yields is a constraint on the *dependency closure* of an
identifier, not on its immediate arguments: identity may depend on the data it
names and on nothing whose definition is still under revision. Note also that
testing an identifier's stability across repeated runs does **not** test this
property. Repeated runs hold the map fixed; the invariance at issue is stability
across a *change* of map, and no fixed-map experiment can observe it.

### 22.5.6 Stability and discrimination are incompatible

The natural specification for an identifier over a tree asks for two things at
once. They cannot both be had.

**Definition 22.5.6.** An *identity assignment* is a family
$\mathrm{id}_T : V(T) \to \mathcal{I}$ indexed by trees. It is

- **reshuffle-stable** if for every sibling permutation $\pi$,
  $\mathrm{id}_{\pi T}(\pi v) = \mathrm{id}_T(v)$ — an identifier follows its
  node through a reordering;
- **discriminating** if $\mathrm{id}_T(u) \neq \mathrm{id}_T(v)$ whenever
  $u \neq v$ are distinguishable.

**Theorem 22.5.7 (elementary).** Let $u \neq v$ be siblings whose subtrees are
equal as labelled ordered trees. Then every reshuffle-stable identity assignment
satisfies $\mathrm{id}_T(u) = \mathrm{id}_T(v)$.

*Proof.* Let $\tau$ transpose $u$ and $v$. Because their subtrees are equal,
$\tau T = T$ as a labelled ordered tree — swapping indistinguishable subtrees
changes nothing — and under that identification $\tau u = v$. Stability gives
$\mathrm{id}_T(u) = \mathrm{id}_{\tau T}(\tau u) = \mathrm{id}_T(v)$. $\square$

**Corollary 22.5.8.** Reshuffle stability and discrimination are jointly
satisfiable **iff** no two content-identical siblings are distinguishable. Where
the observation alphabet separates them — for instance by exposing position —
no identity assignment has both properties.

*Proof.* Theorem 22.5.7 forces equal identifiers; discrimination forbids them
exactly when the pair is distinguishable. $\square$

The corollary relocates the question. Which identifier to use is *not* an
independent design choice to be settled by comparing candidates: it is determined
by what the observation alphabet separates. If position is observable, no
candidate satisfies both properties and searching for one is wasted effort. If it
is not, content-keying satisfies both and the matter is closed.

**Proposition 22.5.10 (identity separates only what its inputs separate)
(elementary).** Let $\mathrm{id} = h(x_1, \dots, x_k)$ for a function $h$. If two
instances agree on every $x_i$, they receive the same identifier however else
they differ. Hence the partition induced by $\mathrm{id}$ is coarser than or
equal to the one induced by the input tuple.

*Proof.* $h$ is a function. $\square$

**Corollary 22.5.11 (the degenerate-input trap).** If some input is
$\mathrm{hash}(y)$ for a field $y$ that is *empty or otherwise constant* across a
class $C$ of instances, and the remaining inputs are also constant on $C$, then
$\mathrm{id}$ is constant on $C$: the whole class collapses to one identifier.
$\square$

The corollary is stated separately because such an input does not look
degenerate. A content hash is the most discriminating input available — *except
exactly where content is absent*, where it is maximally constant. An identity
scheme keyed on a content digest therefore has its worst behaviour on the
instances that carry no content, which are also the instances least likely to be
noticed. Whether that class is empty is an empirical question about the
extractor, not a property of the identity function.

**Remark 22.5.9 (the third option the dichotomy hides).** Theorem 22.5.7 says
content-identical siblings share an identifier. It says nothing about how many
there were. Identity and multiplicity are different questions, and a
representation that records the second is not obliged to discriminate in the
first: by PROP-10.2 an extraction is a *multiset*, so multiplicity is part of the
datum rather than a property of identifiers. Collapsing identical siblings to one
identifier is lossless if and only if their count is retained; it is lossy if the
representation is a set. This is the only escape from 22.5.8 that does not
require changing what is observable.

This reverses the usual reading. An invariant is normally treated as something a
representation *has*; Prop. 22.4 says it is something a representation *incurs*,
by storing what it could derive. A representation with no derived components has
no invariants of this kind — its consistency is a tautology rather than a
property.

The cost is exact and is the recomputation of $f$. Where $f$ is cheap the trade
is unambiguous; where $f$ is not, the invariant is the price of the cache and
should be recognised as such rather than discovered later.

### 22.6 Parallel indexing

**Proposition 22.6 (elementary).** The unzip map
$u : (A \times B)^{*} \to A^{*} \times B^{*}$ is injective and, for
$|A|, |B| \geq 1$ and sequences of length $\geq 2$, not surjective. Its image is
exactly the length-matched pairs.

*Proof.* Injectivity: $u$ preserves length and each position, so the pair of
projections determines the original sequence. Non-surjectivity: $(\langle
a\rangle, \langle b_1, b_2\rangle)$ has unequal lengths and no preimage.
$\square$

So representing a correspondence as two parallel sequences enlarges the carrier
beyond the correspondences themselves, and the surplus — unequal lengths and
mismatched positions — is exactly what an alignment invariant excludes.
Representing it as one sequence of pairs makes that surplus undenotable, and by
22.4(2) the alignment predicate becomes a tautology.

### 22.7 What a set of violated predicates identifies

The natural conjecture is that violations propagate along derivations: if
$c_2 = f(c_1)$ and $c_3 = g(c_2)$, a violation of the first predicate should
force a violation of the second. **This is false.** Take $c_2 \neq f(c_1)$ but
$c_3 = g(c_2)$: the first predicate fails, the second holds. The predicates
constrain different pairs, and nothing links their truth values.

What does hold is a statement about *reads*, not about derivations.

**Definition 22.7.** For a predicate $P$ on $C$, let $\mathrm{read}(P) \subseteq
\{1,\dots,n\}$ be a set of components such that $P$ is invariant under changes
to every component outside it.

**Proposition 22.8 (elementary).** Let a mutation $m$ change only components in
$K$. Then
$$\{\, P : P \text{ holds before } m,\ fails\ after \,\} \;\subseteq\;
\{\, P : \mathrm{read}(P) \cap K \neq \emptyset \,\}.$$

*Proof.* If $\mathrm{read}(P) \cap K = \emptyset$ then $m$ changes no component
$P$ reads, so by Definition 22.7 the truth value of $P$ is unchanged. $\square$

**Corollary 22.9.** The set of predicates a state violates identifies, at most,
the components that were mutated — and among predicates with the same read-set
it cannot discriminate at all. It carries no information about which violation
was "the cause", because the notion is not expressible in these terms.
$\square$

The containment in 22.8 is generally proper: a predicate may read a mutated
component and still hold, if the mutation happens to preserve its condition.
Localisation therefore requires read-sets, which the predicate statements do not
carry unless recorded, and even with them narrows only to a set of components.

## 23. Change across the diagram

**Proposition 23.1.** Let $S=(\!(\phi)\!)$ and let $T'$ differ from $T$ by an edit
at $v$. Then $S'(u)=S(u)$ for all $u\notin\mathrm{anc}(v)\cup\{v\}$.

*Proof.* By induction on the tree order: $S(u)$ depends only on $\lambda(u)$ and
the children's summaries (Prop. 5.3.1), and if $u$ is neither $v$ nor an ancestor
then $v$ lies in no child subtree of $u$, so by hypothesis every $S(c)$ is
unchanged. $\square$

**Corollary 23.2.** The recomputation set of an edit is the zipper context at $v$
(Thm 5.5.1): a located change *is* an element of $\partial F$, so the repair set
is given, not searched. $\square$

**Proposition 23.3.** Propositions 23.1–23.2 concern catamorphisms only. A
quantity that is a function of a $\sim_g$-class rather than of a subtree — such
as the commutation count of §12.3 — is **not** a catamorphism, and an edit at $v$
may change it at nodes arbitrarily far from $v$ in the tree order.

*Proof.* $|[v]_{\sim_g}|$ depends on the whole class, and by Prop. 19.2 the class
is determined by addresses across the document; inserting one member changes the
count for every member. $\square$

Proposition 23.3 delimits the reach of Prop. 23.1 exactly: locality of repair is a
consequence of the *fold*, and any quantity defined on the *quotient* escapes it.
The two are visible in Part II as $\mathcal{T}$-valued versus
$V/{\sim_g}$-valued, and the distinction is structural.

**Proposition 23.4.** A Merkle digest (§7.2) is a catamorphism into a monoid whose
operation determines its blindness: if children are combined commutatively the
digest is invariant under sibling permutation, and by Prop. 23.1 a pure
reordering then alters no digest on the root path.

*Proof.* Commutativity makes the child combination a function of the multiset of
child digests. $\square$

**Corollary 23.5.** Under a commutative combination, digest equality certifies
equality of the *multiset* of children, not of the sequence; under a
position-dependent combination it certifies the sequence. Neither is uniformly
preferable, and the choice determines what counts as a change. $\square$
